![Figure 1](figures/1_london_ta_trend.png)

**Figure 1. Households in temporary accommodation arranged by London boroughs, September 2019 to March 2026.**

*Notes:* Number of households living in temporary accommodation (TA) arranged by London borough councils under homelessness legislation, measured on the last day of each quarter. 'Households with children' and 'nightly-paid accommodation' are subsets of the total, not additional households. Nightly-paid, privately managed, self-contained accommodation is booked by the night from private providers and is generally the most expensive form of TA for councils. Figures are MHCLG's London regional totals, which include MHCLG's imputed values for boroughs that did not submit data in a given quarter. Households placed outside London by a London borough are included.

*Source:* MHCLG, Statutory homelessness in England, detailed local authority tables, Table TA1 (H-CLIC).

![Figure 2](figures/2_ta_rate_by_borough.png)

**Figure 2. Households in temporary accommodation per 1,000 households, by London borough, 31 March 2026.**

*Notes:* Households in TA arranged by each borough divided by MHCLG's estimate of the total number of households in that borough. The dashed line is the rate for London as a whole. Households are counted by the borough that owes them the homelessness duty, not by where they are living: a household placed by one borough in accommodation in another (or outside London) counts towards the placing borough. City of London has very few households, so its rate is sensitive to small changes in numbers.

*Source:* MHCLG, Statutory homelessness in England, detailed local authority tables, Table TA1 (H-CLIC).

![Figure 3](figures/3_real_rents_vs_pay.png)

**Figure 3. Real private rents and real full-time pay in London, 2015 to 2025 (index, 2015 = 100).**

*Notes:* Rent is the mean monthly private rent across all property sizes in London from the ONS Price Index of Private Rents, taken in April of each year to match ASHE's April reference date. Pay is gross annual pay of full-time employees living in London at the 25th percentile, median and 75th percentile of the London pay distribution. All series are deflated by CPIH for April and indexed to their own 2015 value, so the chart compares growth since 2015, not levels (see Figure 4 for levels). PIPR covers rents on all existing private tenancies, not only new lets, so it adjusts gradually and rises more slowly than advertised rents when the market is rising. Pay in 2020 and 2021 is affected by the pandemic: furlough and job losses concentrated among lower-paid workers changed the mix of people in work, which can raise measured pay, and ONS advises caution when comparing these years. The 2020 peak should be read with this in mind.

*Source:* ONS, Price Index of Private Rents (PIPR), October 2026 release; ONS, Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE), resident analysis, via Nomis; ONS, CPIH index (series L522).

![Figure 4](figures/4_rent_share_of_pay.png)

**Figure 4. Average London private rent as a percentage of one full-time salary, by pay level, 2015 to 2025.**

*Notes:* Annual rent (12 times the mean monthly PIPR rent for London in April) divided by the gross annual pay of a single full-time employee at the 25th percentile, median or 75th percentile of the London resident pay distribution (ASHE, April). Both are in cash terms, so inflation cancels out of the ratio. This compares the average rent for all property sizes with one gross salary, so it is not a household affordability ratio: households may have more than one earner or receive benefits, may rent smaller or cheaper homes than the average, and pay is shown before tax. Levels are therefore higher than in ONS's private rental affordability statistics, which use modelled household income; the chart is intended to show the trend and the gap between pay levels. The dotted line marks the 30% threshold commonly used to define affordable rent.

*Source:* ONS, Price Index of Private Rents (PIPR), October 2026 release; ONS, Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE), resident analysis, via Nomis; ONS, CPIH index (series L522).

![Figure 5](figures/5_reasons_for_homelessness.png)

**Figure 5. Households in London owed a homelessness duty, by selected reason for loss of last settled home, quarterly, June 2020 to March 2026 (quarter ending).**

*Notes:* Households assessed by London boroughs as owed a prevention duty (threatened with homelessness within 56 days) or a relief duty (already homeless), added together, by the main reason for losing or being at risk of losing their last settled home. Each household is counted in the quarter of its assessment, so these are flows of new cases, not the number of households in TA. Three reasons are shown: the end of a private assured shorthold tenancy, the most common type of private letting; having to leave accommodation provided by the Home Office to people seeking asylum, which happens once a claim has been decided (for example, after a person is granted refugee status); and family or friends no longer being able to provide accommodation, shown for comparison as a long-standing leading reason. Other reasons (such as domestic abuse or the end of a social tenancy) are not shown. Low private-tenancy figures in 2020 and early 2021 reflect Covid-19 restrictions on evictions in England, which were fully lifted at the end of May 2021. MHCLG rounds these regional figures to the nearest 10.

*Source:* MHCLG, Statutory homelessness in England, detailed local authority tables, Tables A2P and A2R (H-CLIC).

![Figure 6](figures/6_why_private_tenancies_ended.png)

**Figure 6. Households in London owed a homelessness duty after the end of a private assured shorthold tenancy, by reason the tenancy ended, quarterly.**

*Notes:* Breakdown of the 'end of private tenancy' series in Figure 5 (prevention and relief duties combined) by the recorded reason the tenancy ended. 'Landlord wishing to sell or re-let' covers landlords ending the tenancy in order to sell the property or let it again; the data do not record whether a re-let was at a higher rent. 'Rent arrears caused by a rent increase' is the specific case where the tenant fell into arrears after the rent went up. 'All other reasons' includes other rent arrears (difficulty budgeting, falls in income, benefit changes, changes in circumstances), breach of tenancy, tenants who complained about disrepair, illegal eviction, abandonment and reasons not known. Arrears following a rent increase are a small share of cases throughout; the largest single recorded reason is the landlord wishing to sell or re-let. Rounded to the nearest 10 by MHCLG.

*Source:* MHCLG, Statutory homelessness in England, detailed local authority tables, Tables A2P and A2R (H-CLIC).
